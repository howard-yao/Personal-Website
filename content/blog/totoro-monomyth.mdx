_If one day, no one in this world cares about you anymore, believe that the rain is the tears the clouds cry for you. And when you feel lost, remember that the flowers blooming by the roadside are still cheering you on._

In Hayao Miyazaki's _My Neighbor Totoro_, two seemingly opposite ideas - fantasy and reality, are woven together with quiet skill. There is no death-defying adventure here, no hero racing to save the world. And yet, within the ordinary rhythms of country life, the film tells a story of growth on the grandest scale. Borrowing the shape of Joseph Campbell's "Hero's Journey," _Totoro_ follows two children through separation, illness, and the slow work of growing up, and through them tells us something simple: growing up isn't about saying goodbye to childhood wonder. It's about holding onto the ability to see magic, even after you've come to understand how the world really works.

![](/blogs/totoro-cn/1.png)

## I. Those Who Can See Wonder — The Call to Adventure

There is no hero saving the world in the world Miyazaki builds, no sweeping epic. The story begins with something as ordinary as moving house. Because their mother is in the hospital, Satsuki and Mei move to the countryside with their father. Faced with an unfamiliar place, a house falling apart at the seams, and worry over their mother's illness, the two girls don't retreat into fear. Instead, they meet everything around them with wide, curious eyes. The soot sprites in the attic, the acorns scattered on the ground, the mysterious hollow deep in the woods... All of it becomes a clue in their exploration of the world.

![](/blogs/totoro-cn/2.png)

It's this almost naive curiosity that leads them into Totoro's world.

But Totoro was never a miracle that simply appeared out of nowhere. It's closer to a way of seeing. To an adult, the acorns scattered on the ground are just fruit knocked down by the wind, and the soot sprites are nothing but dust and shadow in a dim corner. To a child, though, they're doorways into another world. Totoro can be found not because he isn't hidden well enough, but because only children are still willing to believe that something beyond the ordinary might be living right beside them.

![](/blogs/totoro-cn/3.png)

A fish is probably the last creature you should ask to describe water. 

In his 2005 Kenyon College commencement speech, David Foster Wallace opens with a fun parable: two young fish swimming along meet an older fish who asks, “Morning, boys. How’s the water?” One young fish replies, “What the hell is water?” 

In much the same way, we grow so used to what surrounds us that we stop noticing the things that matter most. Childhood isn't valuable because it exists in some fairy-tale world sealed off from reality, but because of the ability to find the fairy tale inside reality. The things that once made children stop and stare never really disappear; we just forget them as we grow older. For Satsuki and Mei, Totoro's existence matters, but what matters more is that they still have the eyes to find him.

So the first invitation _Totoro_ extends is this: find again the eyes that can see wonder, and rediscover the beauty of the world inside what has become routine.

![](/blogs/totoro-cn/4.png)

## II. Planting the Seed of Belief — Meeting with the Goddess

The scene that left the deepest impression on me is the one where, on a rainy night, Totoro gives the sisters a handful of seeds.

Mei and Satsuki plant them with such hope, checking every day for a sprout that never comes. Then, one night, the Totoro family gathers in the yard, leaping and calling out under a bright moon. As they move, the sleeping seeds stir, push up through the soil, and shoot skyward until they become a single towering tree reaching into the clouds. The sisters bury themselves in Totoro's soft fur and ride the wind up to the treetop, looking down over the sleeping village as if they'd reached into the future itself.

![](/blogs/totoro-cn/5.png)

When they wake the next morning, the great tree is gone. But the small sprouts poking up through the dirt send the sisters running and shouting, "It wasn't a dream!" Their father just smiles, watching them laugh, as if that quiet, wordless look were its own proof that the furry magician really had come in the night.

Plenty of people still argue over whether that towering tree was a reality mapping or Cinderella's glass slipper. But maybe _Totoro_ was never trying to answer that question in the first place. Because when night ends and the tree vanishes, what's left behind in the soil is a real, living sprout. Whether the dream was real stops mattering. What matters is that something began growing after that night.

Growing up works the same way.

As we get older, we eventually discover that a lot of what we believed as children wasn't real. There's no Totoro in the forest, no Catbus in the sky, and the miracles we once trusted without question seem to fade with the years. But realizing this doesn't mean the fantasy loses its meaning. If anything, it's exactly these fantasies that shape how we come to understand the world. Fantasy was never the opposite of reality, but the reason reality can be looked at with any tenderness at all.

The father in the film understands this completely. When his daughters excitedly tell him about the wonders they've seen, he just listens quietly, as if he already knew the answer all along. For an adult, proving that Totoro doesn't exist rational questioning wouldn't be a hard thing to do. But as a child grows up, protecting the forest inside his/her heart is far harder than exposing the fantasy for what it is. Maybe in one day, his daughters will discover on their own that there's no Totoro in the woods and no Catbus in the sky. But until that day comes, he's willing to let them go on believing that the world holds real wonders.

He pedals his bicycle down the country road with both daughters aboard, holding the family together the way that giant tree once held the sisters aloft. He laughs out loud in the middle of a storm, using his own courage to chase away his children's fear. He quietly carries the weight of a hospitalized wife, of family life stretched thin, of an uncertain future and still leaves room in the sky for his daughters' imagination to run free. Just as Totoro watches over the children in the forest, the father watches over his daughters' childhood.

![](/blogs/totoro-cn/6.png)

To me, _Totoro_ is a story about a gentle kind of inheritance passed between generations.

Children learn to trust the world through Totoro, and adults become Totoro by protecting children's fantasies.

And thus, _Only children can see Totoro. Only adults can become Totoro._

## III. Those Who Walk Through the Storm — Road of Trials

But growing up was never just about discovering beauty.

What makes _Totoro_ so moving, except for how adorable Totoro really, really is 🥺, is that underneath all that gentleness, there's a faint unease running the whole way through. Their mother in the hospital, an illness with no clear answer, an unfamiliar place, and the sisters' quiet fear of losing someone they love. All of it hangs like a shadow at the edges of the story.

At the start of the film, a storm rolls in on their very first day in the new house. Young Mei and Satsuki are terrified by the thunder, but the father just laughs and tells them:

> "If you laugh, the scary things will run away."

So the three of them start laughing together.

![](/blogs/totoro-cn/7.png)

It was not until many years later, I realize the father was never really fearless. A wife in the hospital, two young daughters to raise alone, that weight is far heavier than any storm. But faced with things he couldn't change, he chose not to pass his anxiety and fear on to his children. Instead, through laughter, he showed them that fear doesn't disappear just because we run from it, but we do get to choose how we face it.

Real courage was never about feeling no fear at all. It's choosing to move forward anyway, even when you know exactly how afraid you are.

Later, when the sisters bring their father an umbrella on a rainy night, they meet Totoro waiting at the bus stop. The huge, clumsy forest spirit just stands there in the rain, letting the drops thunder down on the leaf he holds overhead. Faced with the dark and the unknown, he simply stands there in it, alongside them.

![](/blogs/totoro-cn/8.png)

So many of life's hardships don't vanish just because we're brave. Illness doesn't disappear. Goodbyes don't disappear. Loss doesn't disappear. But there will always be someone willing to stand beside us when we're afraid. Sometimes it's a parent, sometimes a friend, sometimes just a stranger who happens to appear at the right moment in our life. It's because they're there that we find the strength to keep going.

And those people are our own Totoro.

## IV. The Price of Growing Up — Ultimate Boon

In the second half of the film, a letter arrives from the hospital saying their mother has taken a turn for the worse, and the story enters its final chapter.

Facing the same fear, Mei and Satsuki respond in completely different ways.

Mei insists on going straight to the hospital to see their mother. Satsuki tries to bury her own feelings, telling herself this isn't the time to burden the adults with more trouble. Their fight looks, on the surface, like a simple disagreement, represents two different forces at work inside the process of growing up.

Mei represents the innocence of childhood. Whatever crosses her mind, she acts on it. If she loves someone, she needs to see them. If she has something to say, she says it. She believes that if she just brings her mother the corn, her mother will get better.

Satsuki, meanwhile, is learning to carry responsibility. She's starting to realize that reality doesn't always bend to what we wish for, and that some things can't be changed no matter how hard we try. So she chooses to hold her feelings in, tucking her worry away where no one can see it.

They seem like opposites, but each of them is an essential part of growing up.

Growing up isn't about abandoning Mei entirely, and it isn't about becoming Satsuki completely either. If we were only Mei, we'd live forever in naivety. If we were only Satsuki, we'd slowly lose ourselves.

The real meaning of growing up is learning to let both of them live inside you at once.

Having the courage to face reality while still holding onto the ability to believe in wonder. Being willing to carry responsibility without forgetting to listen to your own heart.

So when Satsuki finally turns to Totoro for help, the film pulls off its ultimate boom.

Up to this point, it was always Mei chasing after Totoro. Now, it's Satsuki who draws the strength to keep going from that same fantasy.

![](/blogs/totoro-cn/9.png)

It turns out fantasy was never the opposite of growing up.  Quite the opposite, it's one of the forces that holds us up as we face reality.

With the Catbus's help, the sisters finally reach their mother. Hidden up in the branches of a tree, they watch her laughing and talking with their father through the hospital window, and once they see for themselves that she's safe, they can finally breathe easy.

And yet, in the end, they never go inside.

They simply leave the corn behind, quietly, and turn to go.

Mei and Satsuki carry the simplest wish a child can have, and leave that ear of corn on the windowsill. Choosing to walk away instead of walking in, that's the answer growing up gives them.

![](/blogs/totoro-cn/10.png)

They don't give up on fantasy, but they learn to understand reality. They don't stop missing her, but they learn to hold that love quietly inside.

Love doesn't always mean possession, and it doesn't always mean standing right in front of the person you love. Sometimes just knowing they're safe and happy is enough.

In this moment, their growing up is finally, truly complete.

## V. My Neighbor Totoro — Crossing the Return Threshold

At the end of the story, the sisters ride the Catbus home.

The film closes on the moment they say goodbye to it.

![](/blogs/totoro-cn/11.png)

The part of the journey that belonged only to Mei and Satsuki has come to an end.

Someday, they'll leave that little house in the country and head out into a wider world. They'll meet new people, live through new goodbyes, and carry burdens and responsibilities all their own.

Over the long years ahead, so many details of childhood will likely blur and fade. They may never see Totoro again, never ride the Catbus again, may even stop believing that a giant spirit really lives deep in the forest.

But that doesn't mean Totoro is really gone. Because Totoro was never just a spirit in the forest.

He is the father's hearty laugh when the storm comes.

He is the quiet shape standing beside you on a rainy night.

He is the hand held out to you when you've lost your way.

He is the ability to treat the world with tenderness, even once you understand how cruel it can be.

He lives inside every person who is gentle and steady at once, inside everyone who quietly watches over others and never asks for anything back.

When we were children, we were all kids searching for Totoro.

When we grow up, we all eventually become someone else's Totoro.

![](/blogs/totoro-cn/12.png)

---

So _My Neighbor Totoro_ is a story about a fantastical creature, but it's also a fable about love. It tells us that even if the fairy tale isn't real, the days we spent believing in it were. Even if Totoro himself may not exist, the people who once watched over us certainly did.

One day, we'll all reach the midpoint of our lives, having lived through goodbyes, losses, and growing up.

But when that day comes, I hope that you, reading this, will still remember:

The rain is the clouds shedding tears for you, the sunlight is the flowers cheering you on, the bus is a cat with eight legs, and the people around you who are willing to help are all furry Totoros, smiling at you.

## Postscript

_Totoro_ is a very special film to me.

I think everyone has one work like this somewhere in their life, something you keep returning to at different ages, and it always meets you differently each time. For me, _Totoro_ is that film.

Honestly, I've always had a bit of a personal soft spot for this movie.

I didn't grow up with many books or films around me. Most of my early childhood memories have gone hazy by now, except for one thing that's stayed sharp: the old DVD player at my grandmother's house, and a handful of discs left behind by the previous owner. It was in that setting that I watched the very first movie of my life — _Totoro_.

After finishing the whole film, my biggest question wasn't about the plot or the characters, it was wondering what planet's language everyone was speaking. Looking back now, I think that not-knowing is exactly what let me believe in everything on screen without holding anything back. To little me, Totoro wasn't a fantasy. He was just a friend, out there somewhere in the world, waiting to be found.

Among all of Miyazaki's films, this one never seems to get talked about the most. When people list their favorites, they usually reach for _Spirited Away_, _Castle in the Sky_, or _Howl's Moving Castle_. By comparison, _Totoro_'s story is almost plain in its simplicity. No breathtaking adventure, no sprawling world, no complicated plot twists. For a lot of people, maybe the strongest memory the film leaves behind is just that round, fuzzy forest spirit.

But that's exactly what makes _Totoro_ so singular.

Its power doesn't come from some dramatic climax, it comes from the details that are almost impossible to put into words. The light of a summer afternoon. Wind moving through the leaves. The dust the sisters kick up when they run. Their father's laugh. Rain drumming on an umbrella. The brief, honest silences between characters.

It's as if Miyazaki tucked every ounce of his tenderness toward life into these small details.

And so you find that even without analyzing anything, without decoding a single symbol or metaphor, you can still be moved by these ordinary, sincere little moments. You just watch, quietly, and before you know it, you're crying.

And that's already enough.

Thank you for reading this far.

Honestly, anyone willing to spend the time reading all the way to the end — to me, you're my Totoro too.
